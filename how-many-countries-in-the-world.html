How many countries are there in the world?

193, 197 or 249. All three are right — they answer different questions, and only one of them is a question about travel.

The short answer

There are 193 United Nations member states, 197 countries on the list most travellers use, and 249 entries in the ISO 3166 country-code standard. No number is a mistake; each one draws the line in a different place, and the disagreement is about sovereignty and about territories, not about geography.

Which one you should use depends on what you are counting. For a geopolitics essay, 193. For a travel record, 197 is the convention. For collecting territories the way the long-haul clubs do, 249.

Why the UN says 193

193 is a membership figure, not a census of the world. It counts the states that have been admitted to the United Nations — the strictest, cleanest and least arguable definition, which is exactly why institutions use it.

It leaves out places you can very much fly to, sleep in and get a stamp from. Vatican City and Palestine hold permanent observer status rather than membership; Taiwan is not a member; Kosovo is recognized by many states but not seated. None of that changes whether you have been there.

Why travellers say 197

197 is the travel community's working answer: the UN's 193 plus the observer states and the de-facto states that function as countries when you actually go — their own border, their own stamp, their own capital.

It is a convention rather than a law, which is why you will occasionally see 195 or 196 instead, depending on whose list you read and which disputed cases it admits. What matters for a personal record is that the number is stated alongside what it contains.

Why a computer says 249

249 is the count of two-letter codes in ISO 3166-1 — the standard behind country dropdowns, domain suffixes and shipping forms. It includes dependencies and overseas territories: Greenland, Guam, Réunion, the Falklands, Aruba and dozens more.

For a traveller this is not padding. Getting to Svalbard or French Guiana is a real journey, and counting them is a legitimate way to keep score — it is simply a different sport from counting sovereign states.

So how many have you visited?

Your own number needs the same treatment: state the denominator. "Forty-one out of 197" is a fact; "forty-one countries" is a claim waiting to be argued with. Voymark carries the denominator everywhere the numerator goes, and lets you switch between all three definitions whenever you like.

There is a second question underneath, and it is the one that actually changes people's totals: does a layover count? That one is answered on the country counter page, where the six visit kinds live.

Common questions

How many countries are in the United Nations?

193 member states, plus two permanent observer states — the Holy See and Palestine — which are not counted as members. That is where the gap between 193 and the travellers' 197 starts.

Is Taiwan a country?

It governs itself, issues its own passports and stamps yours on arrival, but it is not a UN member and its status is disputed. Travellers' lists generally include it; the UN's 193 does not. Voymark counts it under the 197 and the 249 rules and excludes it under 193 — the setting is yours.

What is the difference between a country and a territory?

A territory has its own borders, often its own currency and stamps, but its foreign affairs belong to another state — Greenland to Denmark, Guam to the United States. That is why they carry ISO codes without being sovereign countries.

Which number should I use for my own travel count?

197 is the safe default: it is what other travellers assume when you say a number out loud. Switch to 193 if you want the strictest possible reading, or to 249 if territories are part of the game for you. Whatever you pick, say it alongside the count.

Has anyone visited every country?

Several people have reached every one of the 197, and a smaller group has gone after the full territory list as well. The reason those claims are always argued over is the one this page is about: without a stated definition, "every country" is not a checkable sentence.
